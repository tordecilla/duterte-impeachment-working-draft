At a glance

  • Acosta's account: Office of the Vice President special disbursing officer Gina F. Acosta testified that she handled four quarterly ₱125 million cash advances and released the cash to security officer Col. Lachica under the Vice President's instruction.
  • The challenge: Prosecution counsel argued that Acosta remained the accountable officer, that transferring the money to an unbonded and non-accountable officer violated the Joint Circular, and that the paper trail did not establish receipt or end use of the cash.
  • The defense: Defense counsel argued that an SDO need not conduct confidential operations or hold security clearance, may rely on a designated security officer's inputs, and may certify after examining and tallying the submitted records.
  • Senator focus: Senator-judges pressed Acosta on fiduciary responsibility, missing or irregular receipts, aliases, the absence of personal verification, the movement of the cash, and whether her reliance on Lachica was sufficient.
  • What's next: The court expected Limuel Ortoño to testify the next day, ordered medical verification of Edward D. Fajarda's condition, subpoenaed three AFP witnesses, and deferred a ruling on the defense's August 12 manifestation.

Senator-judge interventions

Sen. Joel Villanueva examined Gina Acosta's role as the Office of the Vice President's special disbursing officer for confidential funds. He asked what qualifications should matter in selecting an SDO and what she had been told her job was. Acosta said she would choose someone with average knowledge, thinking, and character; as SDO, she was to hold, disburse, and process the related documents and had been directed to read the governing joint circular. She said she did not decide whether confidential funds would be used. Asked to describe the full process, Acosta traced a Vice Presidential request, Department of Budget and Management action, the approved financial plan, a disbursement voucher and supporting papers, check encashment and release to security officer Col. Lachica, followed by liquidation after documents showed the operation had ended. Villanueva then asked who determined the purpose, amount, recipient, timing, and final approval. Acosta said the Vice President made the request, Lachica implemented the operation and received the released funds, and she did not determine the recipient or timing. Villanueva pressed whether she had exercised any independent judgment, including by changing documents after audit observations, notices of suspension, or notices of disallowance; Acosta said her function was to process the papers and maintained she had performed it properly. He contrasted that account with her previous service as an SDO in Davao, where she said there had been no audit observation memorandum, and tested her review of the fund utilization and acknowledgement receipts; Acosta said she checked whether the amounts matched the implementing officer's account. Finally, he asked whether the safe box contained documents matching aliases to real names; Acosta said it had contained only an acknowledgement receipt, which had been submitted to the Commission on Audit and was no longer in the box.

Sen. Francis "Kiko" Pangilinan examined whether Gina Acosta's transfer of confidential funds to security officer Col. Lachica completed her duty as special disbursing officer. He distinguished a transfer to Lachica from a final payment for supplies or medicine and asked how Acosta could ensure the disbursement was proper if she did not know how the confidential funds were spent. Acosta said she remained accountable but relied on Lachica's utilization report and his designation by Vice President Sara Duterte to implement operations. Pressed on whether money could have gone to a personal account, to a named recipient, or to other people, Acosta said she did not know because she was not on the ground. Pangilinan then asked about the reported P122 million for medicines and P112 million for supplies. Acosta said she knew there had been such purchases but could not recall the specific amounts for tree-planting or Lakbay Aral activities, relying instead on utilization reports and acknowledgement receipts; when Pangilinan said documents could be false and could not establish where the money went, she again said she trusted Lachica. When Pangilinan returned to the medicine records, he asked why acknowledgement receipts described medicines worth P122 million as rewards for informers. Acosta said she could not explain because Lachica implemented the operations. Pangilinan said that left the accountable officer unable to say whether medicine had actually been bought, and Acosta said she relied on the documents supplied by the security officer. He then asked about turnovers of 70 or 80 million that Acosta released to Lachica in her extension office on the Vice President's instruction. Acosta said only she and Lachica were inside, leaving them as the only people who could confirm the handover. Pangilinan compared that practice with Acosta's earlier SDO service in Davao and challenged the use of acknowledgement receipts without official receipts or sales invoices in the OVP liquidation reports; Acosta said Davao likewise had no official receipts. In a final follow-up, Pangilinan cited the Joint Circular's requirement that an SDO disburse directly to the final legitimate recipient or purpose and asked whether Lachica was that final recipient. Acosta answered no. Pangilinan said the rule therefore placed the SDO, rather than Lachica, in the position to make the final disbursement, and summarized Acosta's explanation as a lack of access to confidential operations because she did not have security clearance. Acosta said Lachica submitted documents to her.

Sen. Erwin Tulfo asked Gina Acosta about her work before becoming the Vice President's special disbursing officer. Acosta said she had worked for the Davao City council as a stenographic reporter and later supply officer, knew Sara Duterte when she was vice mayor, and became an SDO when Duterte was mayor. Tulfo then asked about names such as Mary Grace Piatos, Chipie McDonald, Pusa, Pagong and Belat. Acosta agreed she had encountered them and did not know Pusa or Pagong as actual surnames, while saying the recipients were real people but the listed names were not their real names. Asked why the records lacked true recipient names and what the safe contained, she said the joint circular did not require the names and identified the CDR as the document stored there. Tulfo said Colonel La Chica lacked a fidelity bond and should not have disbursed funds; Acosta said she followed the Vice President's instruction to release the funds to the designated security officer. She said La Chica's fund-utilization report and object-of-expenditure list matched the amounts and expenses, so she found no improper spending. On Tulfo's challenge to using confidential funds for tree planting, tables and chairs, Acosta said the funds covered confidential surveying and inspection around tree-planting sites and equipment used in operations. When Tulfo resumed, he asked about Acosta's earlier explanation that P3.5 million in tables and chairs served confidential operations. Acosta said the acknowledgement receipts had come from Lachica and that she had not memorized Joint Memorandum Circular 2015-01. Tulfo then read the provisions he said limited confidential funds to purchasing information, surveillance transport, safe-house incidentals, and specialized technical equipment, while prohibiting furniture and general supplies even when they were said to support operations. In a final resumed question, Tulfo gave Acosta a hypothetical in which a security officer returned with conspicuous luxury assets after receiving 100 million pesos and told her not to ask because the matter was confidential. He asked whether she would let that pass or question it; Acosta said she could not answer because she had not experienced that situation.

Sen. Raffy T. Tulfo challenged special disbursing officer Gina Acosta's acceptance of confidential-fund records bearing aliases and a single signature for multiple purported informants. He asked why an accountable officer would not object to names including Bilat, Pagong and Pusa, or to multiple names carrying one signature. Sen. Risa Hontiveros asked whether special disbursing officer Gina Acosta could vouch for the accuracy of acknowledgement-receipt dates, purchases and amounts. Acosta said she relied on security officer Col. Lachica, who implemented the confidential activities. Hontiveros pointed to an acknowledgement receipt for Clarice Hontiveros dated on her own birthday and asked about its unusual detail; Acosta said she did not know it was the senator's birthday and said she had raised and verified receipt issues, while acknowledging that some receipts lacked signatures, names, dates or legible entries. Sen. Panfilo Lacson asked who held fiduciary responsibility for entrusted public funds, and special disbursing officer Gina Acosta said it was hers rather than Col. Lachica's. He examined what happened each time she withdrew 125 million pesos, asking whether the cash first entered her office vault or went directly to Lachica. Acosta said the full fourth-quarter 2022 amount did not enter the vault; in the first quarter of 2023 she released about 80 million pesos to Lachica based on stated expenses, and she released the second- and third-quarter amounts in three tranches each. Lacson also said purchases of supplies still required invoices or official receipts from real persons even if used for confidential activities. Acosta said her reading of the Joint Circular did not require the names or invoices. He then asked whether preparing and signing the physical and financial plan was merely ministerial. Acosta said she acted with the security officer's inputs and discussed the program, while Lacson stressed that treating the role as ministerial would be inconsistent with her fiduciary responsibility.

Sen. Vicente C. Sotto III tested whether the Office of the Vice President's special disbursing officer could delegate her responsibility. Ms. Gina Acosta said she was the person authorized to disburse but had released confidential funds to the security officer; Sotto maintained that the special-disbursing authority itself could not be delegated. He then contrasted the reported distribution across 132 locations nationwide in 11 days with Acosta's statement that the OVP had no regional officers. Acosta said Vice President Sara Duterte had designated Colonel La Chica to implement confidential operations on the ground, but she could not say whether La Chica alone had covered all 132 locations, saying only that he was the OVP's appointed security officer and BPSBG commanding chief.

Sen. Imee Marcos asked whether Vice President Sara Duterte ever handled, saw, or took any part of the confidential funds after Gina Acosta withdrew them and turned them over to Colonel La Chica. Acosta said she had not seen the Vice President do so. Marcos then examined Acosta's responsibility for liquidation and supporting documents, asking whether the Vice President reviewed the hundreds or thousands of records or personally authenticated each acknowledgement receipt. Acosta said she did not; the receipts came from La Chica in a brown envelope, and he was the official who implemented the activities and obtained the receipts. Marcos asked whether Acosta had noticed any suspicious or defective records and whether the Vice President knew of them. Acosta said some receipts lacked names, signatures, dates, or legible entries, which she raised with La Chica, but she had no knowledge that the Vice President knew of the defects.

Bam Aquino pressed special disbursing officer Gina Acosta on when she first saw unusual purported-recipient names, including Piatos, Belat, Pagong and Pusa, and why she did not determine whether they were aliases. Acosta said her review of almost 800 acknowledgement receipts focused on whether expense amounts matched the fund-utilization report and permitted categories, although she had noticed some unusual names.
