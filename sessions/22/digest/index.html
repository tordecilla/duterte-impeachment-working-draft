Witnesses and evidence

Marizza B. Grande, PSA Assistant National Statistician for the Civil Registration Service, described supervising verification and reviewing official replies. She outlined a process of receiving requests, searching the Civil Registry System, consolidating results and securing review and approval.

Grande confirmed that 1,685 of the 2,669 names from the DepEd and OVP lists submitted for verification had no corresponding birth, marriage or death record, approximately 63%. Divinagracia said the submitted names excluded receipts containing only signatures or unreadable names.

Reverification of single-match entries produced 37 death certificates: 32 for names on the OVP list and five on the DepEd list. Grande also identified birth-record matches indicating 20 minors on the OVP list and five on the DepEd list.

Senator-judge interventions

Sen. Raffy T. Tulfo said the contested receipt names might be aliases and that apparent matches to birth certificates could be coincidental. He asked why prosecutors had not traced the parents or guardians of listed one- and two-year-olds through addresses held by agencies such as the LTO, PhilHealth or GSIS, suggesting they might be Department of Education or Office of the Vice President employees who had used their children's names. Tulfo said informants in the military, NBI and PNP may use aliases and asked that Colonel Lachica and the corresponding DepEd officer be called because they were connected to the names used. Prosecutor Mae Sancho Divinagracia said the team did not know where the people were and lacked time to trace them; she said the defense had not identified the names as aliases in its answer or at pretrial. Defense counsel Justin Nicol B. Gular replied that the Commission on Audit's findings had not raised a problem with aliases and that the prosecution still bore the burden of proof. Tulfo said aliases could protect informants' identities, but stressed that the same signature and handwriting appeared across different named recipients, which he said made the receipts highly questionable. The exchange tested alternate explanations for PSA nonmatches against the pattern on the acknowledgement receipts.

02:00:06 · S0069102:00:35 · S0069502:02:57 · S0069902:07:03 · S00726

Sen. Paolo Benigno "Bam" Aquino asked how often Filipinos who are born, marry or die would be absent from the Philippine Statistics Authority's civil-register databases. Marizza B. Grande said the 2020 census put birth registration at 96.6 percent, with an unofficial 2024 estimate of 97 to 97.1 percent; the PSA had also registered about 600,000 births through its project since 2022. She said people missing from birth records were largely indigenous peoples, Muslim Filipinos and residents of geographically isolated or remote areas, which the PSA was reaching through mobile registration. Grande estimated death registration at about 84 percent but said the agency had no study of unregistered marriages; she said the database held about 143 million birth records. Aquino then said the inquiry into names absent from those records mattered only if the names were real. If they were aliases, he said, their absence would be expected; he noted that the defense had not admitted they were aliases and that Gina Acosta had only offered her own view that they were. Aquino urged further inquiry into whether the names were aliases and, if so, the real identities behind them.

03:30:16 · S0103803:30:36 · S01041L303:32:01 · S0104703:32:33 · S0105003:33:54 · S0105703:34:02 · S01058L2

Sen. Erwin Tulfo asked Marizza B. Grande whether the PSA had checked several specified receipt names and whether an absent civil-registry record established that a person did not exist. Grande said the results were in the certifications but she could not recall each name; she could not speculate about a person's existence, and said someone who had not completed delayed registration would receive a negative PSA result. She said the PSA database included records as early as 1904 as well as some much older delayed registrations. Tulfo also asked whether PSA had checked PhilSys. Grande said it had not and said access or disclosure there required a court order. In a later round, Tulfo confirmed that 1,322 Office of the Vice President names submitted to PSA had no birth record and asked the defense whether it maintained that a person without a birth certificate existed. Atty. Justin Nicol B. Gular said that was not the defense position, but said Grande's testimony did not support the inference that a person without a birth certificate did not exist. Tulfo said the court still needed to establish whether the receipt names were real people or aliases with real people behind them. The exchange distinguished the PSA's negative-record results from proof that a named individual did not exist.

03:35:17 · S0105903:35:49 · S0106103:36:36 · S0106503:37:17 · S0106803:37:52 · S0107003:38:37 · S01077L304:05:31 · S0124904:06:25 · S01257

Sen. Joel Villanueva asked PSA statistician Marizza Grande to put the agency's 143 million birth registrations against its 29 million death registrations and compare the 63% of names in this request that were unregistered with ordinary PSA searches. Grande called 63% unusual, said other agency requests did not reach that level, and said the largest she had seen was 30% to 40%. Villanueva then tested the aliases explanation: Grande said PSA could not verify an alias without the person's real identity and, once an alias had been tied to someone, would need identifying information such as date and place of birth and parents' names. She confirmed that, without independently verifiable information connecting an alias to a real identity, PSA could not establish that the person behind an acknowledgement receipt existed. Villanueva said the question was not whether aliases were proper, but what the records could independently verify.

03:48:49 · S0115903:49:59 · S0116703:51:03 · S0117303:52:13 · S0118203:53:20 · S01186

Sen. Risa Hontiveros asked what the Philippine Statistics Authority's civil-registration verification could establish about names on acknowledgement receipts from the Office of the Vice President and Department of Education. Marizza B. Grande said the PSA treated the entries as ordinary verification requests and did not classify the 63 percent with no birth, marriage or death record as either real people or nonexistent people, because it did not know whether they were aliases. Hontiveros asked whether that approach presumed the names were real; Grande said the agency had simply checked them as registered names. Hontiveros contrasted this work with the Alice Guo case, where an existing birth record led the PSA to conduct an investigation and field fact-finding; Grande confirmed that no investigation or fact-finding had occurred for the acknowledgement-receipt names. Hontiveros then asked the defense for a clear position. Counsel said the names were classified information but were aliases, while reserving fuller treatment for the defense evidence presentation. The exchange separated PSA record verification from an investigation into identity and put the defense's alias position on the record.
