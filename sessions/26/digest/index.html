Witnesses and evidence

The Court enlisted retired Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr., who submitted a written memorandum; retired Chief Justice Artemio V. Panganiban; retired Chief Justice Reynato S. Puno; and retired Associate Justice Adolfo S. Azcuna. It asked them whether 'all the members of the Senate' means the full 24-member Senate or only senators legally and physically able to participate and vote; whether Avelino v. Cuenco bears on unavailable senators; and whether a senator-judge who did not substantially participate may vote on the final question.

00:20:37 · S00016

Davide's memorandum said the constitutional framers had presumed that 'members' excluded people unable to exercise senatorial powers because of mental, physical, health, or legal restraints. It therefore urged a denominator of senators qualified to vote and treated Avelino v. Cuenco as not directly controlling in impeachment but potentially useful by analogy. For senators who had not substantially participated, the memorandum left the matter to their conscience and sense of duty.

00:26:25 · S0002600:28:53 · S00027

Panganiban described impeachment as sui generis, with legal and political characteristics. He argued that senators should be guided by truth and fairness, test documents and witness testimony firsthand, hear both sides before judgment, and independently evaluate the evidence. On the threshold, he favored counting senators able to vote; on review, he said Supreme Court intervention would require grave abuse of discretion.

00:37:12 · S0003200:46:21 · S0004500:47:45 · S0004700:48:13 · S0004803:33:30 · S0030700:48:49 · S00050

Puno framed the wording as supporting more than one school of constitutional interpretation and argued for a contextual rather than purely literal approach. He described ordinary Senate impeachment actions as political questions, while identifying a narrower justiciable category when a respondent's right to a fair and impartial tribunal is violated. On detained participation, he raised an open question whether courts and the Senate could reconcile their jurisdictions by allowing a detained senator-judge to perform that temporary judicial duty, potentially including remote participation.

Azcuna argued from the constitutional history of the supermajority requirement that it was designed to make conviction difficult. In his view, the denominator consists of all existing senators rather than those present: a senator may be unable to vote yet still count unless dead, resigned, or removed. He said making the threshold variable could permit manipulation by lowering it when senators are prevented from participating.

04

Senator-judge interventions

Senator-Judge Juan Miguel "Migz" F. Zubiri asked whether the Senate had violated the Constitution when it concurred in four treaties with 13 votes after vacancies and an official mission. Retired Chief Justice Artemio V. Panganiban answered that it had not: in that example 20 senators remained, 13 was two-thirds, and treaty concurrence differed from an impeachment trial.

02:54:28 · S0021702:58:13 · S0021902:58:40 · S0022003:00:27 · S0022403:01:38 · S0022503:02:05 · S00228

Senator-Judge Joel Villanueva asked the three amici how to read "all the members" when the Constitution says neither 16 nor those present; whether a detained senator remains a member for the denominator; and what neutral rule could prevent either a manufactured veto through absence or an artificial majority through exclusion. Retired Chief Justice Reynato S. Puno said the language supports competing interpretations and invited consideration of why and how a detained senator might perform the judicial duty. Panganiban focused on whether a senator can know the evidence well enough to render a truthful and fair judgment. Retired Associate Justice Adolfo S. Azcuna distinguished the ability to vote from inclusion in the denominator, said current membership remains the denominator absent death, resignation, or removal, and suggested a law requiring permission for detained senators to participate.

03:04:04 · S0023303:05:02 · S0023503:05:32 · S0023603:06:44 · S0023903:07:22 · S0024003:09:08 · S0024603:11:04 · S0025203:15:11 · S0026103:18:02 · S0026803:18:55 · S0026903:20:16 · S0027103:20:59 · S0027203:22:23 · S0027503:23:28 · S0028103:24:45 · S0028503:25:25 · S0028603:27:00 · S00294

Senator-Judge Erwin Tulfo asked why the Philippine text uses two-thirds of all members rather than the United States formulation of those present, whether a fixed 16 vote should remain when senators are involuntarily unavailable, who has the final interpretive authority, and whether the Constitution needs fine-tuning. Azcuna said the Philippine wording was a deliberate design to make conviction difficult, while Panganiban said senators able to participate should not be immobilized by an unreachable threshold. Puno argued for reading the provision with the Constitution's other guarantees; he described impeachment as generally political but due-process violations as potentially justiciable. On Tulfo's closing question, Azcuna said the Supreme Court has final authority on whether Senate action complies with the Constitution; Panganiban said no constitution is perfect; and Puno said the 1987 Constitution is not perfect and contains an amendment process.

Senator-Judge Sherwin T. Gatchalian asked whether the Senate has authority to interpret the provision, whether the status of detained or missing senators is a political question, how truth and fairness bear on participation, when a contextual reading is appropriate, and whether the Court may revisit the Presiding Officer's threshold ruling. Puno said the Court ordinarily decides political questions but that a respondent's right to fair and impartial judges supplies a due-process limit. Panganiban said a senator unable to know the evidence should not vote; Azcuna said excluding a senator who remains in office from the denominator could invite Supreme Court intervention. Puno said a contextual reading is warranted when the text supports multiple interpretations, and Panganiban said a quorum may reconsider a temporary ruling before a final judgment.

Senator-Judge Panfilo M. Lacson asked whether consulting the amici demonstrated prudence against a later grave-abuse challenge; whether a fixed all-24 threshold still makes sense if nine senators are stranded abroad; and when the Senate's threshold interpretation becomes justiciable. Panganiban called the consultation prudent, favored allowing two-thirds of the available 15 to act rather than making accountability impossible, and said Supreme Court intervention turns on grave abuse of discretion rather than ordinary disagreement. Azcuna answered that, under his fixed-threshold view, failure to obtain 16 would result in acquittal even without an affirmative acquittal vote.

Senator-Judge Francis Pangilinan asked whether changing the Presiding Officer's threshold ruling during the proceeding would deny either party due process. Panganiban said there was no implemented rule or vote yet. Puno said the Court was still determining the provision's meaning, not changing a rule, and that any Supreme Court challenge was not ripe without a final interpretation and demonstrated injury. Azcuna agreed that the issue was procedural, for which there is no vested right and which may apply to a pending case.

04:51:03 · S0059404:52:23 · S0059604:55:06 · S0060304:56:25 · S0060804:57:13 · S0061104:58:26 · S0061704:59:04 · S00619

Senator-Judge Risa Hontiveros asked whether senator-judges who took the oath but then continuously stayed away should still count, how to treat a senator who did not take the oath, whether a fixed all-24 denominator could protect an impeached president who caused senators to be detained without basis, and why impeachment power belongs to the Senate. Panganiban said a nonparticipating senator should not be in the denominator and should voluntarily desist from voting. Puno said the circumstances and the oath's function matter, and pointed to the Avelino test for persons beyond the Senate's coercive jurisdiction. Azcuna defended the fixed threshold even at the cost of an occasional failure of accountability, because a variable denominator can be manipulated toward a simple majority; he described impeachment as a legislative, hybrid political-and-judicial check.

Senator-Judge Robinhood "Robin" Padilla asked whether truth and fairness must begin with the Court and its members, how to reconcile the Senate Impeachment Court's authority with the Sandiganbayan's control over detained senators, whether recordings could permit later participation, and whether the constitutional words may be changed without popular ratification. Panganiban agreed that truth and fairness applies to everyone. Puno described conflicting jurisdictions that may be reconciled, including temporary participation by a detained senator-judge. Azcuna said the current Senate rules require face-to-face participation but may be changed, while detained senators still count in the denominator; he distinguished constitutional amendment, which requires the prescribed process and plebiscite, from interpretation. Puno confirmed the three amendment routes, and Padilla urged the Court to consider changing its own rules to protect members' attendance rather than changing the threshold.
